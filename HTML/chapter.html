CHAPTER I
1857

The uprising of 1857 did not appear from nowhere.

For decades, the East India Company had expanded its control across large parts of India. Political annexations, economic pressure, changes to established social structures and growing resentment among soldiers created an atmosphere of deep distrust.

Among the Company's Indian soldiers, or sepoys, dissatisfaction had been growing. The immediate spark came from controversy surrounding new rifle cartridges that soldiers believed were greased with cow and pig fat — deeply offensive to both Hindu and Muslim religious beliefs.

On 10 May 1857, Indian soldiers at Meerut rebelled. They marched towards Delhi, where they sought the authority of the ageing Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar.

Delhi became an important centre of the uprising. The rebellion then spread to other regions, including Kanpur, Lucknow and Jhansi.

Leaders from different backgrounds joined the struggle, each driven by their own grievances and ambitions.

The fire of resistance had spread far beyond the barracks where it began.

In Jhansi, Rani Lakshmibai emerged as one of the most enduring figures of the rebellion. In Kanpur, Nana Sahib became a major leader.

Tantia Tope continued the resistance through military campaigns, while Bahadur Shah Zafar became the symbolic figure around whom the rebels could unite.

The British eventually suppressed the uprising through military force and the support of allied Indian rulers and forces.

Delhi was recaptured, resistance centres were defeated, and the rebellion gradually came to an end.

The uprising failed to remove British power from India. But its consequences were enormous.

In 1858, the rule of the East India Company came to an end and authority was transferred directly to the British Crown.

The fire of 1857 had been suppressed, but the desire for freedom had not disappeared.

WHY IT MATTERS

A rebellion that changed everything.

The uprising of 1857 was the first large-scale challenge to British East India Company rule across several parts of India.

Although it was suppressed, it changed British policy and became an enduring symbol of resistance in India's later struggle for independence.
